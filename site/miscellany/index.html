A Commendation from the Austin Police

April 2, 1974

On March 25, 1974, Steve and a friend named Ronnie Inascore watched a man rob someone on the street in Austin, and followed him. They flagged down Officer Wolsch, directed him to 703 East Sixth Street, gave him a description of the man, and warned him the subject was armed with a knife. The offender was identified, arrested and charged, and the money was recovered and returned to the victim.

A week later the Chief of Police wrote to him about it.

Dear Mr. Walker:

… As the results of your interest and action the offender was identified, arrested and charged and the money that he had taken was recovered and returned to the victim.

You are to be commended for your cooperation and assistance and you are being awarded a Certificate of Appreciation for your actions.

Sincerely,
R. A. Miles, Chief of Police

Two things make the letter worth keeping. It is addressed to Mr. John Walker, which was his legal first name and one most people never knew. And years later, when he handed the letter to a friend named Barry, he wrote across the bottom of it in his own hand:

“Barry, just a little something to let the man know I’ve changed my wicked ways.”

Which brings us to the wicked ways.

The Outlaw Years

Three stories, recorded for his oldest daughter

Late in his life Steve recorded a set of stories for his oldest daughter, Kat, that he called, himself, his outlaw stories. He asked that they stay inside the family until after he was gone. They are here now, in his voice, lightly cleaned from the recordings.

I. The airplane run

About 1978.

All right, listen. I wanted to tell my daughter a story from my outlaw days. This goes back to, I guess we could say, 1978, more or less. It was airplane smuggling.

It started out wrong, the whole damn thing. I didn’t know the plane. It was a twin-engine, the kind they used to call the doctor-killer. The flight was all the way down to Salina Cruz, in Oaxaca, and when we got there we got the load we were looking for, but the gasoline was subpar and we barely got out of there. Both engines were popping and cutting out, and we had mountains to go over. It was a close thing, and tiresome.

To make matters worse, we were flying into a place in central Texas, east of Waco, a town called Marlin, I believe. This trip was unknown to me. The people there were unknown to me. We landed the airplane, we unloaded the load, and the police showed up. The pilot, myself, and three of the folks we knew who were on the ground, we got out of there. It was such a close thing that the headlights on the lawmen’s cars were shining on us as we took off. They were that close.

So the next problem is, where do we put this airplane? It was a rental out of Austin, a place called Ragsdale. No sense complicating matters, so we flew it back into Ragsdale. Of course it’s two o’clock in the morning, which is not normal, so there’s going to be a night watchman. But we had no choice. What do we do with him?

Generally, situations like that are where I took control. I got money from each of the guys in the plane. As they walked off to the cars, the night watchman moved up on us, and to this day it’s the most amazing thing. The kid was young, maybe twenty-three or twenty-four, and he moved quietly and quickly. Cat-like. It was unnerving how fast he moved. I interceded with him. I looked at him and said, “Here’s four hundred dollars.” I handed him the money and said, “Give us fifteen minutes before you call anybody, and I’d appreciate it.” He took it. We got in the car and left.

Everybody scattered after that. We were back in Austin, in different places, worried about what would happen with that airplane, whether they’d check it, because of what we’d brought up from Oaxaca. I’m sitting there reading the Austin American-Statesman, and they’ve just that day caught a serial rapist who’d been terrorizing the town for a month or so. They’d named him the One-Punch Rapist. His method was to wait after dark in the parking lots around the campus, come in without hesitation, and knock out his victim with one punch. They’d be out cold. He was obviously very strong. Then he’d assault them and run off, and the poor victim, not only assaulted but semi-conscious. He was getting more and more violent. The police were worried that if he kept it up, it would end in a death.

And I’m reading the paper, they’ve caught him, they’ve caught the guy, and I read further. He was the night watchman at Ragsdale, that private airstrip there in Austin.

He was the man I’d handed the money to so he wouldn’t call the police. I didn’t know who he was. As it turned out, I wouldn’t even have had to give him anything. He pocketed it and didn’t say a word, and we were free and clear. Thankfully, he was caught. It was pure serendipity that the man our fate depended on that night was him.

The point, I guess, is that you’ve got to stay calm, but you’ve also got to have what that takes.

II. The pistol at the roadblock

A story about situational awareness.

I’m driving down a Mexican highway to the place where I had the mule ranch. I’d been running Number Nine Outfitters for several years. It was what you’d call a financial windfall, but it kept me busy, you know what I’m saying.

Over the years I’d made many friends among the people who do this, including the Presidente Municipal, the mayor, of the little town of Zaragoza in southern Nuevo León. I’d bought him a Llama .380 semi-automatic pistol. It was his special, he’d asked for it, and he even wrote me a letter giving me permission to carry the pistol back to him. That would come in handy.

So I’ve got this pistol in my truck and I’m driving in the state of Tamaulipas, just before you get into the big mountains. I go around a corner and there’s a roadblock. Judicial police, the plainclothes guys. Very, very serious. Always very serious.

Now, the cars with a mom and dad and the kids were being waved on, but any vehicle with one or two single fellows in it was getting pulled over and checked. I’m looking at the situation and realizing this pistol isn’t hidden, it’s just in my luggage, and it’s still illegal. Never mind that I have a letter from the mayor of Zaragoza. It’s still something you don’t do.

So I’m watching them check these guys, and they are going through them. And if there’s one thing I learned in Mexico, it’s that there’s no habeas corpus. You don’t stand there and go, “I’m an American citizen, you can’t treat me that way.” That’s where you get, as they say, five across the ass.

So it comes my turn. This guy comes up, and I’ll never forget two things about him. He spoke perfect English, and he had two gigantic screwdrivers, big enough to pull apart just about any door frame.

He comes up and says, “Do you have anything to declare?” I looked at him and said, “Yes, I do.” He said, “What is it?” I said, “I’ve got this Llama .380 semi-automatic pistol.” That took him aback. Because here’s my thinking: what you don’t want to do with Mexican law is lie to them, particularly when you know you’re probably going to get caught with something illegal. Just go ahead and tell the truth. At least you’ll save yourself a beating. You might still go to jail, but at least you won’t have insulted them by lying. That’s my point.

He said, “You’ve got a Llama .380?” I said, “Certainly.” I opened the little briefcase I kept in the truck and handed it to him, still in the box, never opened, never fired. He looked at me and said, “So what are you going to do with this, sell it?” I said, “No, no, it’s already paid for. I’m taking it to the man I bought it for.” And I handed him the letter from the mayor of Zaragoza, not that it would have meant anything to the federal judicial police.

He says, “You know these people?” I said, “Yes, I’ve been in there a long time.” He says, “What is it you do?” I said, “I take people on mule rides in the mountains.” He named a couple of the pueblos back in the middle of it. I said, “Yes, sir, I pass through them all the time.” He looked at me and smiled, and he said, “I was born in one of them.” Then he said, “Listen, hide this pistol a little better and get the hell out of here.” I thanked him, put the pistol back in the case, and drove on.

The point being, if I had lied to him and they’d found it, it wouldn’t have mattered if I’d had a letter from the President of Mexico. Stay straight, and don’t lie to Mexican cops.

III. The poachers at the airstrip

The next story is again from my outlaw period. We were in Texas, at a ranch with an airstrip, waiting for an airplane to arrive. I’m sitting there with my partner, who’s got the radio. The plane was maybe twenty-five or thirty minutes from landing. And as soon as we quit talking to him, through the gate comes an unknown pickup truck, parks right at the end of the runway, and turns off its lights. My partner says, “Oh my God, is this the law?”

I looked at the way they moved. This was not law. This was probably poachers, or a young couple looking for a place to nest.

I said, “Give me five minutes before you call this off, or we go to plan B,” which would have been something, no telling how stupid. I walk toward them with my flashlight, cowboy hat on, jacket on, shining the light on the ground in front of me, not at them, so they’ll know I’m coming. I don’t want to sneak up on anybody. I get up to the car, and sure enough, it’s two poachers, and they’re embarrassed. I said, “I’m not worried about what you’re up to, but people are about to be here, and if they hear a rifle shot they’ll call the game warden. So leave now and nobody will say anything.” They said, “Thank you, sir, thank you,” and drove away.

No sooner were their headlights out of range than the plane showed up. Everything went perfect. But that was an iffy moment.

Anyway, your dad loves you. Bye.

Mules for the Mujahideen

The 1980s

Steve kept a mule ranch at Zaragoza, in Nuevo León, and ran as many as a hundred head. In the 1980s that business led somewhere unlikely. He told The Brownsville Herald that the mule connection put him in the position of supplying mules to the United States government for the Afghan fighters resisting the Soviets, sourcing the animals out of Zacatecas and selling them to purchasing agents in Piedras Negras.

His reasoning was pure Steve, and entirely practical: Zacatecas mules were mountain-hardened, born and worked at altitude on broken ground, unlike animals out of Colorado or Tennessee. If you were sending mules to the Hindu Kush, you wanted mules that already knew that kind of country.

This one comes from a single newspaper interview and the family has not independently confirmed it. It is recorded here as he told it.

A Letter from a Texas Historian

June 22, 1980

In the summer of 1980 the historian Malcolm D. McLean wrote Steve a two-page letter. He had to send it care of Steve’s father, because Steve had written to him without including a return address on the letter or the envelope, and the postmark only said Waco.

The letter is a small window onto a stretch of his life that is otherwise thinly documented. He was farming and ranching in Mexico, and McLean congratulated him on it while adding a word of caution: take on a Mexican partner, so that if the government ever expropriated the property, he would lose too, and the risk to Steve’s investment of time, money and labor would be greatly reduced.

He was also lobbying, at thirty-one years old, to change United States policy on Mexican labor. McLean told him that any such change would have to come at the national level and pointed him to Congressman Jim Wright of Fort Worth, whose views, he said, were very similar to Steve’s.

And he was giving talks. McLean closed with a note about one of them:

In your talk on “The Altiplano, Key to the Future of Texas,” be sure to work in a sentence about General Ignacio Zaragoza, who was born in Goliad, Texas, educated in Tamaulipas and Nuevo León, and won the Battle of Puebla for the Mexicans against the French on the Cinco de Mayo. There are literally thousands of schools, plazas, and streets in Mexico, and a number in Texas, named for him. Mention of his name always gets an enthusiastic response.
